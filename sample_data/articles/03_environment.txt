The Long Life of a Plastic Bag

Plastic was invented to make daily life lighter, cheaper, and more hygienic, and in that sense it has succeeded almost too well. The world now produces hundreds of millions of tonnes of plastic every year, roughly half of it for products that are used once and thrown away, such as packaging, straws, and carry bags.

A plastic shopping bag is used for minutes but can persist in the environment for centuries, gradually breaking into ever smaller pieces rather than truly disappearing. Enormous amounts of this waste reach the ocean, where circulating currents gather it into vast floating patches. Sea turtles mistake drifting bags for jellyfish, and dead whales regularly wash ashore with stomachs full of plastic film.

As debris fragments, it becomes microplastic: particles smaller than five millimeters that have now been found everywhere from mountain snow to table salt and drinking water. Scientists have detected microplastics in human blood, although their long-term health effects are still being studied. That uncertainty is itself unsettling to many researchers.

Recycling, long promoted as the answer, handles only a small share of the problem. Different plastics melt at different temperatures, food contamination ruins whole batches, and shipping waste to distant sorting plants is costly, so most plastic has historically been landfilled, burned, or dumped. Environmental groups therefore argue for reducing waste at the source instead. Dozens of countries have banned or taxed single-use bags, shops increasingly offer refill stations and cloth alternatives, and designers are learning to make products meant to be repaired rather than replaced.

Beach and river cleanups organized by volunteers remove tonnes of litter each year and, just as importantly, photograph and count what they collect, building the public pressure behind new laws. Individual choices matter too, and carrying a reusable bag or bottle seems trivial only until a whole neighborhood adopts the habit. The plastic age will not end quickly, but its litter does not have to be its longest-lasting legacy.
