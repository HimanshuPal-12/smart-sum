Why Sleep Is the Cheapest Medicine

Doctors keep returning to the same short list of advice: eat sensibly, move daily, and sleep enough. Of the three, sleep is by far the most neglected. Surveys across many countries suggest that roughly one in three adults regularly sleeps less than the recommended seven to nine hours, and many people wear their tiredness as a badge of honor.

During deep sleep the brain files away memories, clears out waste products, and rehearses skills learned during the day. The body uses the same quiet hours to repair muscles, regulate hormones, and strengthen the immune system. Cut the night short, and every one of these housekeeping jobs is interrupted.

The costs of chronic short sleep are well documented. People who consistently sleep less than six hours show higher rates of obesity, high blood pressure, heart disease, and type-two diabetes. Reaction times slow until they resemble those of a mildly drunk driver, which is why drowsy driving is now treated as a serious road-safety issue. Mood suffers too, since sleep-deprived people are more anxious, more irritable, and more prone to depression.

Fortunately, most sleep problems respond to simple habits rather than medicine. Going to bed and waking up at the same time every day, including weekends, anchors the body clock. A cool, dark, and quiet bedroom helps, as does avoiding coffee after mid-afternoon. Bright screens before bedtime delay the release of melatonin, the hormone that signals darkness, so sleep specialists suggest putting phones away during the hour before bed.

Persistent snoring, long sleepless stretches, or daytime exhaustion despite a full night in bed can signal disorders such as sleep apnea, which deserves a doctor's attention rather than another cup of coffee. In an age of expensive treatments and complicated health advice, sleep remains the one remedy that is genuinely free. Protecting it may be the single most effective health decision most people will ever make.
