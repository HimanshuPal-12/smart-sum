Long before container ships, goods moved by camel, yak, and porter along the trail network known as the Silk Road, connecting China, Central Asia, India, Persia, Arabia, and the Mediterranean for some fifteen hundred years. Caravans carried silk but also spices, tea, porcelain, glass, paper, horses, stones, dyes, and herbs, with bales changing owners many times between origin and distant markets. The ideas traveling the same paths proved more consequential: papermaking spread west and changed how knowledge was stored, numerals, astronomy, and medicine moved among India, the Islamic world, and Europe, and religions such as Buddhism traveled with merchants — historians call the network the ancient world's internet. Crossroads cities grew rich on trade, though the routes also carried the plague; the system declined when ocean shipping became cheaper, leaving desert cities to the sand. Archaeologists still uncover their ruins, and modern trade initiatives invoke the routes as symbols of exchange, not isolation, as the engine of progress.
