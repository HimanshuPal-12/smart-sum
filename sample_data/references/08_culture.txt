Festivals are a community's memory made visible, encoding history, values, and hopes through food, color, and music, with the knowledge behind them traditionally passed from grandparents to children through repeated practice rather than formal teaching — a living inheritance carried in songs, dance steps, and stories. That chain is thinning as families move to cities and elders stay behind, while commercial sponsors reshape celebrations around sales and broadcasts replace participation with viewing; a tradition that is only watched, folklorists warn, is halfway to a museum exhibit. Preservation efforts are growing on several fronts: universities record songs and craft techniques from the oldest practitioners, museums digitize costumes and oral histories, and some schools invite artisans to teach weaving, drumming, or kite-making. Carefully managed cultural tourism can finance revival by giving young people economic reasons to master abandoned skills, though successful revivals keep the community at the center. A festival survives only while it still means something to those who celebrate it, and meaning transfers by doing, not display.
