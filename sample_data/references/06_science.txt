Telescopes placed above Earth's atmosphere escape the distortion that makes stars twinkle, recording sharp images of faint, distant objects. They also detect infrared light that the atmosphere blocks, revealing cool stars, dusty regions where planets form, and galaxies whose light has stretched red over billions of years. Space observatories have measured the universe's expansion, watched planetary systems being born, captured light from the earliest galaxies, and used the tiny dip when a planet crosses its star, along with spectroscopy, to estimate exoplanet atmospheres and search for water and methane. These missions are built by international consortia, and their observations are archived and opened to the public after a short exclusive period, letting students and amateur astronomers analyze the same data as professionals — some have earned co-authorship by spotting anomalies. Larger next-generation telescopes are planned, and history suggests each leap in sensitivity brings unexpected discoveries, from dark energy to planets around ordinary stars.
